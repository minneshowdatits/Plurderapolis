Overdose: Recognize & Respond

This is the page to read before you ever need it. Overdoses are survivable when someone nearby knows what to do. That someone can be you.

Happening right now? Let me walk you through it

Tap through — 30 seconds, plain language, no judgment. Stay with them while you do this.

The short version

  • Can't wake them up and breathing is slow, shallow, or stopped — call 911 right now. Don't wait to be sure.
  • Blue or gray lips and fingernails, gurgling or snoring sounds while unconscious — treat it as an overdose.
  • Naloxone (Narcan) cannot hurt someone who isn't overdosing. When in doubt, use it. (In someone physically dependent on opioids it can trigger sudden withdrawal — miserable but not life-threatening, and still better than an overdose.)
  • Minnesota law protects people who call 911 during an overdose. Make the call.

What to do, step by step

0 of 5 done

Say "someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally" and give the address. You don't have to say what they took — describe symptoms. Stay on the line; dispatch will coach you.

Call 911 now

Tilt head back, lift chin, pinch nose, breathe into mouth — one breath every 5 seconds. If you know CPR, do chest compressions hard and fast in the center of the chest. Don't know how? Dispatch talks you through it. Any breathing help beats none.

Nasal spray: peel the package, place tip in one nostril, press the plunger firmly. No improvement in 2–3 minutes? Give a second dose. It wears off in 30–90 minutes — they can overdose again after it fades.

Roll them onto their side, top knee bent to keep them stable, head tilted slightly back. Keeps the airway open if they vomit.

Talk to them even if they're out — hearing is the last sense to go. You did the right thing by staying.

What NOT to do

Myths that waste time or hurt people:

✕ Cold water / ice baths

Doesn't reverse overdose. Causes shock, wastes time.

✕ "Walk them off" / slapping awake

They can't walk it off. Shaking doesn't fix breathing.

✕ Leave them to "sleep it off"

People die this way. Alone is the most dangerous place to overdose.

✕ Make them vomit

Choking risk, zero benefit.

✕ Give more drugs to "balance out"

Uppers don't cancel downers. This kills people.

✕ Wait to be sure

Narcan can't hurt someone who's not overdosing. When in doubt, act.

Opioid or stimulant? They look opposite

  • Can't wake up, slow or stopped breathing, blue lips, limp, tiny pupils
  • Give naloxone — it reverses opioids
  • Rescue breathing + 911 + stay
  • Overheating, extreme agitation or paranoia, racing heart, chest pain, seizures — the opposite picture
  • Cool them down: shade, cool water on neck and wrists, fan them. Overheating is the main killer.
  • Calm the environment: dim lights, fewer people, quiet voice
  • Call 911 for chest pain, seizures, extreme overheating, or unresponsiveness. Same Good Samaritan protections.
  • Naloxone won't help a pure stimulant emergency — but it won't hurt. When in doubt about what was taken, the 911 call is still the move.

Minnesota's Good Samaritan law — are you protected?

Tap through — most people are more protected than they think (MN Statute 604A.05, sometimes called Steve's Law).

1. If I call 911 in good faith during an overdose, can I get limited immunity from certain drug possession charges?

Yes — for both the caller and the person overdosing, when you give your name, stay until help arrives, and cooperate. You don't have to be the first caller.

2. Does it also protect the person who overdosed?

Yes — the same limited immunity covers them. The incident also can't be used to revoke probation or parole.

3. Does it protect dealing, warrants, or other crimes?

No — dealing isn't protected, and it doesn't stop police acting on warrants or other crimes. Since Aug 2023, though, MN doesn't criminalize paraphernalia possession at all, even with residue.

We're harm reduction volunteers, not lawyers — but the practical advice never changes: always call.
