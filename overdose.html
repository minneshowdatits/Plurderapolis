Overdose: Recognize & Respond

This is the page to read before you ever need it. Overdoses are survivable when someone nearby knows what to do. That someone can be you.

The short version

  • Can't wake them up and breathing is slow, shallow, or stopped — call 911 right now. Don't wait to be sure.
  • Blue or gray lips and fingernails, gurgling or snoring sounds while unconscious — treat it as an overdose.
  • Naloxone (Narcan) cannot hurt someone who isn't overdosing. When in doubt, use it. (In someone physically dependent on opioids it can trigger sudden withdrawal — miserable but not life-threatening, and still better than an overdose.)
  • Minnesota law protects people who call 911 during an overdose. Make the call.

The 4 signs

You don't need a medical degree. Check these, in order:

  1. Won't wake up. Shout their name, shake their shoulders hard, rub your knuckles firmly on their breastbone. Someone who's just sleeping will react. Someone overdosing won't.
  2. Breathing is wrong. Watch their chest. Slow, shallow, irregular — or stopped entirely. Gurgling, snoring, or choking sounds while unconscious are a red flag, not "sleeping it off."
  3. Color change. Lips, fingernails, or skin turning blue, gray, or ashy — especially around the mouth. On darker skin, check the lips, gums, and nail beds.
  4. Limp body. Completely unresponsive, muscles slack, pupils tiny (pinpoint) in opioid overdose.

If you see these, stop reading and act. Everything below is the detail — the action is: call 911 first, then help.

What to do, step by step

  1. Call 911 first. Before anything else. Say "someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally" and give the address. You don't have to say what they took — describe symptoms. Stay on the line.
  2. Rescue breathing if they're not breathing. Tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose, and breathe into their mouth — one breath every 5 seconds. If they're not breathing normally and you know CPR, start chest compressions: push hard and fast in the center of the chest, with rescue breaths if you're trained. If you don't know how, dispatch will talk you through it. Any breathing help is better than none.
  3. Give naloxone (Narcan). It's a nasal spray: peel open the package, place the tip in one nostril, press the plunger firmly. That's it. If there's no improvement in 2–3 minutes, give a second dose. Naloxone wears off in 30–90 minutes — the person can overdose again after it fades, which is why staying until medics arrive matters.
  4. Recovery position if they're breathing but unconscious. Roll them onto their side, top knee bent to keep them stable, head tilted slightly back so the airway stays open. This keeps them from choking if they vomit.
  5. Stay. Until help arrives. Talk to them even if they're out — hearing is the last sense to go, and it's also for you. You did the right thing.

Free Narcan and test strips are available all over Minnesota — see our resources page for where to get them. Narcan is also sold over the counter at pharmacies now, no prescription needed. Carry it. It weighs nothing and it saves lives.

What NOT to do

  • No cold water, ice baths, or ice down the pants. It doesn't reverse an overdose — it causes shock and wastes time.
  • Don't try to "walk them off" or slap them awake. An overdosing person can't walk, and shaking doesn't fix breathing.
  • Don't leave them alone "to sleep it off." People die this way. Alone is the most dangerous place to overdose.
  • Don't try to make them vomit. Choking risk, zero benefit.
  • Don't give them more drugs (uppers to "balance out" downers, etc.). This kills people.

Stimulant overamping looks different

Not every drug emergency is an opioid overdose. Too much stimulant ("overamping") looks like the opposite: overheating, extreme agitation or paranoia, racing heart, chest pain, seizures.

  • Cool them down — move to shade, cool water on neck and wrists, fan them. Overheating is the main killer here.
  • Calm the environment — dim lights, fewer people, quiet voice. Don't crowd them.
  • Call 911 for chest pain, seizures, extreme overheating, or if they become unresponsive. Same Good Samaritan protections apply.
  • Naloxone won't help a pure stimulant emergency — but it won't hurt either. When in doubt about what was taken, the 911 call is still the move.

Minnesota's Good Samaritan law

The fear that stops most people from calling 911 is "will I get in trouble?" In Minnesota, the answer is: the law is on your side. Minnesota's Good Samaritan law (sometimes called Steve's Law, MN Statute 604A.05) gives limited immunity from certain drug possession charges for both the person who calls 911 in good faith during an overdose and the person overdosing. You don't have to be the first person to call — give your name, stay until help arrives, and cooperate, and the protection applies.

It doesn't cover everything — dealing isn't protected, and it doesn't stop police from acting on warrants or investigating other crimes — but simple possession at the scene of a rescue is, and the incident can't be used to revoke probation or parole. Since August 2023, Minnesota doesn't criminalize possessing drug paraphernalia at all, even with residue, and test strips and syringes aren't legally paraphernalia in the first place. Don't let fear of a possession charge stop you from saving someone's life. When you call, simply describe the symptoms; dispatch cares about the medical emergency, not busting you. (We're harm reduction volunteers, not lawyers — but the practical advice never changes: always call.)
