Mental Health & the Scene

The highs, the crashes, the community, the comedowns: let's talk about it honestly, without judgment and without the "just stop going out" lecture.

The short version

  • Feeling wrecked on Tuesday after a big weekend is usually chemistry, not character. Give it 48–72 hours before you judge your life.
  • If the low mood sticks around for weeks, that's different, and it's treatable. That's not weakness, that's a health issue.
  • Substances can trigger or amplify anxiety and panic. It doesn't mean you're broken.
  • You don't have to white-knuckle it alone. Queer-friendly, low-cost help exists in Minnesota.

Where are you at right now?

A quick check-in, not a diagnosis, just a nudge toward the part of this page that fits. Answer honestly; nobody sees this but you.

1 · Understand

The comedown blues

If you've ever cried in the shower on a Tuesday for no clear reason after a heavy weekend, you're not uniquely messed up: you're experiencing something extremely common. Stimulants and empathogens temporarily flood your brain with feel-good chemicals, and the bill comes due a few days later: flat mood, irritability, anxiety, that hollow "what's the point" feeling. Add sleep deprivation and dehydration, and of course you feel like garbage.

That's not your real baseline: it's a depleted brain running on fumes. Sleep, food, water, sunlight, and a couple of days will do more than spiraling will.

The rule: no big life conclusions during comedown week. Don't quit your job, don't text your ex a novel, don't decide you're a failure. Revisit everything in a week with a rested brain.

1 · Understand

When it's more than a comedown

A rough few days after partying is normal. Feeling persistently empty, hopeless, or numb for two weeks or more is not "just a comedown," nor is losing interest in everything, struggling to get out of bed, or having thoughts of harming yourself.

Depression and anxiety are medical conditions, not moral failures, and they respond to treatment, including for plenty of people in this scene who'd never admit it out loud.

Visual weirdness that lingers for days after tripping, like static, trails, or halos around lights, can be HPPD (hallucinogen persisting perception disorder). It is uncommon and usually fades, but if it is distressing, bring it up with a doctor honestly. Our psychedelics guide explains it in plain language.

If you're in crisis right now, skip the rest of this page: call or text 988, or text HOME to 741741. Both are free, confidential, and available 24/7.

2 · Steady yourself

Anxiety, panic, and the scene

Stimulants, caffeine, nicotine, sleep deprivation, and overwhelming crowds are basically a recipe for anxiety, and comedowns can serve up panic attacks days later. Chest tight, heart racing, brain screaming that something is terribly wrong: most of the time, that's anxiety. It peaks and passes.

What helps in the moment: slow your breathing (in for 4, out for 6), cold water on your face or wrists, name 5 things you can see. Step outside or into a chill space if you can. Our bad trip guide walks through grounding step by step.

2 · Notice

When partying becomes avoidance

No judgment here, just an honest question worth sitting with: are you going out because you love it, or because the week is unbearable and Saturday is the only time you feel okay? There's a difference between a scene you love and a scene you're hiding in.

Signs worth noticing: dreading the week starting Sunday night, needing to be intoxicated to enjoy events you used to love sober, or feeling like your "real life" only happens at shows. Noticing it is the whole battle, most people never get that far. And it's information, not shame, a good reason to talk to someone.

3 · Get support

Finding help in Minnesota (that actually gets you)

Therapy only works if you can be honest, so finding the right therapist matters, especially if you're queer, trans, kinky, poly, or otherwise not living a default life. A therapist who flinches at your lifestyle isn't going to help you. A few starting points:

  • Psychology Today's therapist finder lets you filter by LGBTQ+ affirming, sliding scale, and specialty. Read profiles: the good ones say outright that they're sex-positive and affirming.
  • Open Path Collective offers sessions in the $40–$70 range for people without great insurance. Real therapists, real help, survivable prices.
  • University counseling centers often offer low-cost therapy to students and sometimes the public.
  • Peer support is real support. SMART Recovery and Refuge Recovery meetings (some secular, some Buddhist-inspired) exist around the Twin Cities for people who want community without a clinical setting.

Our MN resources page has crisis lines, queer and trans support orgs, and more, all local.

3 · Show up

Showing up for a friend

When someone tells you they're struggling, you don't need to fix them. Mostly don't make it weird. Listen. Believe them. Skip "just cheer up" and "have you tried yoga." Try "that sounds really hard" and "I'm glad you told me."

Offer concrete help, not open-ended help: "want me to sit with you Thursday?" beats "let me know if you need anything." And know your limits: you're their friend, not their therapist. If they're talking about harming themselves, stay with them and call or text 988 together.
