Safer Snorting & Route Safety

No lectures. Your nose is delicate machinery and most people treat it like a dumpster. Here's how to do less damage.

The short version

  • Grind it fine. Chunks tear tissue. Powder doesn't.
  • Your straw is yours alone. Sharing spreads hepatitis C, even with people you trust.
  • Alternate nostrils. Rinse with saline afterward.
  • Your nose needs rest days. If it's constantly raw, that's it asking for a break.

1 · Protect

The sharing thing is the big one

Let's lead with the part most people don't know: hepatitis C spreads through shared straws, bills, and bumpers. It lives in microscopic blood: the kind you can't see on a rolled bill that just went through someone's irritated nasal tissue. It survives on surfaces for up to 3 weeks depending on conditions. "I've shared with them before" is not a test result.

Hep C is now curable: modern treatment is pills taken for 8–12 weeks, with very few side effects and a cure rate over 95%, and plenty of people carry it for years without symptoms while it quietly damages their liver. All of that is avoidable with a 10-cent plastic straw of your own.

  • Use your own straw, every time. Cut one to pocket size, or grab a pack of short plastic straws. Some harm reduction orgs hand them out free.
  • Don't share the pile either: if someone's bloody straw touched the shared surface, assume contamination and move on.
  • Get tested. Hep C testing is quick and often free at clinics and syringe service programs. Knowing beats wondering.
2 · Care

Grind fine, go gentle

Chunks and crystals have sharp edges. Dragging them through your nasal passages causes micro-tears: that's where the burning, the bleeding, and the long-term damage start. A fine, even powder is dramatically easier on the tissue.

  • Crush thoroughly on a clean, hard surface. Take the extra 30 seconds.
  • Sniff gently: you don't need to inhale like a vacuum. A soft sniff deposits it where it absorbs; a hard rip sends it to your throat and stomach.
  • Alternate nostrils to spread the wear instead of destroying one side.
  • Don't blow your nose hard right after: you'll just expel what you took and irritate everything further.
2 · Care

Nose care (yes, really)

Your nasal tissue is some of the most blood-rich, delicate tissue in your body, and it does not bounce back fast. A little maintenance goes a long way:

  • Saline rinse or spray after. Clears residue, soothes irritation, keeps things moist. Drugstore saline spray costs a few bucks.
  • Humidify dry air, because winter in Minnesota plus stimulants is brutal on sinuses. A humidifier at home helps more than you'd think.
  • Take rest days. Tissue needs time to recover. Back-to-back nights are where chronic damage builds.
  • Don't mix uppers nasally in one night: the combined vasoconstriction starves the tissue of blood flow, which is how necrosis starts. Check combos on our interaction checker too.

3 · Know the risks

Myth or fact?

Five quick ones, straight from this page. Tap Myth or Fact and see how you did.

1. Sharing a straw can spread hepatitis C, even if you trust the person.

Fact. Hep C lives in microscopic blood and survives on surfaces for up to 3 weeks. Trust isn't a test result.

2. Sniffing harder gets you higher, faster.

Myth. A hard rip just fires it into your throat and stomach. A soft sniff is what actually gets absorbed.

3. A saline rinse afterward actually helps your nose recover.

Fact. It clears residue, soothes irritation, and keeps the tissue moist, cheap and easy.

4. You'd know if you had hep C, the symptoms are obvious.

Myth. Plenty of people carry it for years with no symptoms while it quietly damages the liver. Get tested.

5. Injecting carries the highest risk of all the routes.

Fact: overdose, abscesses, blood-borne infections. If you inject, never share equipment; MN syringe service programs give out free sterile supplies, no judgment.

3 · Know your options

Routes, compared honestly

No route is safe. They all carry risks. But they're not equal, and knowing the tradeoffs is harm reduction:

Gentlest on the body
  • Slower onset, longer duration, no nasal damage
  • Watch out: easier to overdo because you get impatient waiting for it to kick in
Faster, harder, costlier
  • Hits faster and harder, which is exactly why it's easier to redose compulsively
  • Wrecks nasal tissue over time (see the routine above)
  • Shares the hep C risk through shared straws and surfaces
Hard on lungs
  • Harsh on lungs and tends to encourage compulsive redosing
  • Sharing pipes and mouthpieces carries its own infection risks
Highest risk
  • Overdose, abscesses, blood-borne infections, by far the most dangerous route
  • Never share equipment. MN syringe service programs provide free sterile supplies, no judgment, see our resources page

Whatever route you use: test your stuff (fentanyl test strips are free at many MN orgs), start small with any new batch, and don't use alone.
