Safer Snorting & Route Safety

No lectures. Your nose is delicate machinery and most people treat it like a dumpster. Here's how to do less damage.

The short version

  • Grind it fine. Chunks tear tissue — powder doesn't.
  • Your straw is yours alone. Sharing spreads hepatitis C, even with people you trust.
  • Alternate nostrils. Rinse with saline afterward.
  • Your nose needs rest days. If it's constantly raw, that's it asking for a break.

The sharing thing is the big one

Let's lead with the part most people don't know: hepatitis C spreads through shared straws, bills, and bumpers. It lives in microscopic blood — the kind you can't see on a rolled bill that just went through someone's irritated nasal tissue. It survives on surfaces for days. "I've shared with them before" is not a test result.

Hep C is now curable, but treatment is expensive and unpleasant, and plenty of people carry it for years without symptoms while it quietly damages their liver. All of that is avoidable with a 10-cent plastic straw of your own.

  • Use your own straw, every time. Cut one to pocket size, or grab a pack of short plastic straws. Some harm reduction orgs hand them out free.
  • Don't share the pile either — if someone's bloody straw touched the shared surface, assume contamination and move on.
  • Get tested. Hep C testing is quick and often free at clinics and syringe service programs. Knowing beats wondering.

Grind fine, go gentle

Chunks and crystals have sharp edges. Dragging them through your nasal passages causes micro-tears — that's where the burning, the bleeding, and the long-term damage start. A fine, even powder is dramatically easier on the tissue.

  • Crush thoroughly on a clean, hard surface. Take the extra 30 seconds.
  • Sniff gently — you don't need to inhale like a vacuum. A soft sniff deposits it where it absorbs; a hard rip sends it to your throat and stomach.
  • Alternate nostrils to spread the wear instead of destroying one side.
  • Don't blow your nose hard right after — you'll just expel what you took and irritate everything further.

Nose care (yes, really)

Your nasal tissue is some of the most blood-rich, delicate tissue in your body, and it does not bounce back fast. A little maintenance goes a long way:

  • Saline rinse or spray after. Clears residue, soothes irritation, keeps things moist. Drugstore saline spray costs a few bucks.
  • Humidify dry air — winter in Minnesota plus stimulants is brutal on sinuses. A humidifier at home helps more than you'd think.
  • Take rest days. Tissue needs time to recover. Back-to-back nights are where chronic damage builds.
  • Don't mix uppers nasally in one night — the combined vasoconstriction starves the tissue of blood flow, which is how necrosis starts. Check combos on our interaction checker too.

See a doctor if:

  • Frequent nosebleeds that are getting worse, not better
  • A whistling sound when you breathe (can mean septum damage)
  • Crusting or sores that won't heal after weeks off
  • Noticeable loss of smell

Be honest with the doctor about what you've been doing. They've heard it a thousand times, they can't help what they don't know, and they're not cops.

Routes, compared honestly

No route is safe — they all carry risks. But they're not equal, and knowing the tradeoffs is harm reduction:

  • Oral is generally the gentlest on the body: slower onset, longer duration, no nasal damage. The tradeoff is it's easier to overdo because you get impatient waiting.
  • Snorting hits faster and harder, which is exactly why it's easier to redose compulsively — and it wrecks nasal tissue over time.
  • Smoking is harsh on lungs and tends to encourage compulsive redosing.
  • Injecting carries the highest risk by far: overdose, abscesses, blood-borne infections. If you inject, never share equipment — our resources page lists Minnesota syringe service programs that provide free sterile supplies, no judgment.

Whatever route you use: test your stuff (fentanyl test strips are free at many MN orgs), start small with any new batch, and don't use alone.
